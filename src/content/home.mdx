Try it with a string of sixteen letters, <Seq s="PHHPPHHPPHHPPHHP" />. Place the letters one at a
time, each on a free node of the grid next to the one before. Two H's that end up
side by side without being neighbours in the string make a **contact**. Make as many contacts as
you can. The best fold has 8.

## The HP model

The game you just played is the **HP model** of protein folding, due to <Cite k="dill1985theory" />. A protein is
a chain of amino acids. Some of them are hydrophobic (H): they avoid water, so in water the chain
folds until they huddle together inside. The others are polar (P) and do not mind water. The model
keeps only this effect. A **fold** puts the string on the cubic lattice, the 3D grid of points with
integer coordinates, with consecutive letters on neighbouring nodes and no node used twice. A fold
is better the more contacts it has.

If you reached 8, look at your H's: they fill a 2×2×2 cube, and every P is outside it. There is no
other way to reach 8: eight nodes of the grid have at most twelve neighbouring pairs, and only a
cube has twelve. Four of those pairs are neighbours in the string already, which leaves 8 contacts.
Keep this picture of a cube of H's with the P's outside, because the whole proof is built on it.

## Why the best fold is hard to find

For a string this short, a computer can try every fold. For longer strings this stops working,
because the number of folds grows exponentially: a string of 100 letters has more than $10^{60}$
of them. Many possibilities alone do not make a problem hard, though. A road map also has
exponentially many routes between two towns, yet a shortest one is found in a fraction of a
second, because it can be built from shortest routes to the towns along the way. Folding seems to
have no such shortcut, and <Cite k="berger1998protein" /> proved a precise version of this.

<Theorem kind="Theorem" source="Berger & Leighton 1998" status="cited">
Given an HP string and a number $m$, deciding whether the string has a fold on the cubic lattice with at least $m$ contacts is NP-complete.
</Theorem>

The theorem asks a yes-or-no question about $m$ rather than for the best fold, because that is the
form the theory of NP-completeness deals with. Finding the best fold is at least as hard, since it
answers the question for every $m$ at once.

NP-complete means two things. First, folding is in **NP**: a proposed answer can be checked in
polynomial time, that is, in a number of steps bounded by a fixed power of the length of the
string. For folding this is easy to see. Given a fold, we walk along it, check that no node is used
twice and count its contacts, a few steps per letter. So the hard part is finding a fold with $m$
contacts, not checking one. Second, every problem in NP can be translated quickly into folding.
Berger and Leighton show this by translating bin packing into folding. Bin packing was already
known to be NP-complete, so every problem in NP translates into it, and from there into folding. A
fast method for folding would therefore solve every problem in NP fast. Whether that is possible is
the P versus NP question, open since 1971, and most researchers believe it is not.

## How the proof works

Bin packing gives us some items, each with a size, and some bins of equal size, and asks whether
the items fill the bins exactly. For each such puzzle Berger and Leighton write down one HP string
with a cube's worth of H's, and they set $m$ to the number of contacts it has when its H's fill a
cube. As with your string of sixteen letters, no other shape of the H's reaches $m$. Call a fold
**perfect** when its H's fill a cube. They build the string so that it has a perfect fold exactly
when the items fit.

The P's do the work. In a perfect fold every P lies outside the cube, so an H between two P's needs
two neighbours outside the cube and can only sit on an edge or at a corner. An H with a P on one
side sits on the surface, and only an H with no P beside it can go inside. By choosing where the
P's go, Berger and Leighton turn filling the cube into a jigsaw in which almost every piece has one
place: rods of lone H's along the edges, ribbons of doubled H's on the faces, and one long run of
H's inside. On one face, the front face, some ribbons run straight across as walls and cut out
strips that act as the bins. The only freedom left is which strip each item's ribbon goes into, so
the string folds perfectly exactly when the items fit.

[The original proof](original-proof/) goes through the argument in fifteen cards. Each opens on
the idea of its step in words, with the exact statement and the proof behind it.

## Is there a simpler proof?

Berger and Leighton's proof is long. It writes out the intended fold in coordinates and needs six
routing lemmas to join its pieces. <Cite k="demaine2012geometric" /> lists a simpler proof as an
open problem, and the survey by <Cite k="istrail2009combinatorial" /> calls the known hardness
proofs "quite involved".
