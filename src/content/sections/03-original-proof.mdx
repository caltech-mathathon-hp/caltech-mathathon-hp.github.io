<Theorem kind="Theorem" source="Berger & Leighton 1998" status="cited">
Given an HP string and a number $m$, deciding whether the string has a fold on the cubic lattice with at least $m$ contacts is NP-complete.
</Theorem>

To show that folding is hard, <Cite k="berger1998protein" /> show that a fast method for folding would also solve bin packing, a puzzle already known to be NP-complete. For every bin packing puzzle they write down one HP string and a number $m$ such that the string has a fold with at least $m$ contacts exactly when the items fit. Such a translation is called a reduction. Together with the fact that a fold is quick to check, it makes folding NP-complete.

The board below tells their argument in fifteen cards. Read the captions in order for the whole story: first how the string is built, then the two directions, a perfect fold gives a packing and a packing gives a perfect fold. The technical steps sit in three stacks that open in place. Open a card for the idea of its step in words; the exact statement, the proof and our notes are under "The details".

The dots on a card say how hard its step is to check, from one to three. Three steps get three dots. In [8 Walls run straight](#step-bl-walls) a footnote of the paper is false as written, and the claim needs another argument. [10 Folding the frame](#step-bl-frame) and [13 Untangling the linkers](#step-bl-crossbar) are long, and the paper leaves many of their details to the reader.

### Why the cube is so big

Even small instances give a large cube. With two bins of size at most 8 (at most 4 if some number is odd, since everything is then doubled), $q = 2\max(K, \lceil B^{1/3}\rceil) = 4$, so $n = q(2q+1)+2 = 38$: 54,872 H's and about 85,000 letters. The running example of the proof cards has bins of size 10, which already needs $q = 6$ and $n = 80$, that is 512,000 H's. It is among the presets, but slow on older computers. The longest pieces are the linkers: each has $52n+2$ P's, far more than it needs, and here each folds its surplus into a small box of P's under the cube.

### How close this is to the paper

The string is exactly that of Fig. 3, checked letter by letter, and the frame, the periods and the spaced rod sit at the coordinates of §3.2.3 (Figs. 4–8). Two kinds of route are only asserted in the paper, so we had to choose them:

- The short hops of four or seven P's between faces are found by a small search that keeps them at distance at least 2 from the cube.
- The linkers do not use the switchboard of [13 Untangling the linkers](#step-bl-crossbar). With a dozen of them it is enough to give each linker its own plane in front of the cube. The paper uses up the surplus length with a loop away from the cube; here it is folded into a small box of P's under the cube, which keeps the picture compact.

Every fold is checked as it is built: its letters are those of $S_B$, no site is used twice, the H's fill the cube, and the H–H pairs reach $3n^2(n-1)$. The panel on the left lists these checks.

Under "The details", the cards also use the paper's terms: an **H–H pair** is two H's on neighbouring nodes, consecutive in the string or not; a **lone H** is an H of a rod; and the **skeleton** is the set of corners and edge nodes.

## Pairs and contacts

Berger and Leighton count every pair of H's on neighbouring nodes, including pairs that are consecutive in the string (p. 31), and the exact statements under "The details" do the same. The rest of this site counts contacts, which leave consecutive pairs out. In every fold of a string, the two counts differ by the number of <Seq s="HH" /> in the string, which the string fixes. So asking for at least $m$ pairs is the same as asking for at least $m$ minus that number of contacts, and the theorem holds with either count.

## Our audit notes

We checked every step against the paper. The note at the end of each card says what we added, corrected or could not find there. Page numbers refer to the RECOMB '98 proceedings, pp. 30–39. In short:

- [2 Why bin packing](#step-bl-problems): a proof that bin packing with even sizes stays NP-complete with small numbers, which the paper only asserts.
- [3 Why a cube](#step-bl-cube): the paper's uncited "classic result" is the Loomis–Whitney inequality, and its equality case needs one more condition.
- [4 Spelling the string](#step-bl-string): the construction needs $B$ even, which the paper does not say.
- [5 The counts match](#step-bl-local-rules): the paper shows that the H's of <Seq s="HPPH" /> are neighbours only on faces; we check edges and corners too, and state two rules the paper uses but does not state.
- [6 Two edges stay free](#step-bl-skeleton): a count in place of the paper's short argument for the corner rod.
- [7 One length per edge](#step-bl-free-edges): the edge with the spaced rod, and the bound $2q+8 < n$ in place of $2q+6 < n$.
- [8 Walls run straight](#step-bl-walls): footnote 7 makes a claim that is false as written, but the conclusion holds for another reason.
- [9 Reading off a packing](#step-bl-packing): a check of the paper's "without loss of generality" for the ends of the core.
- [10 Folding the frame](#step-bl-frame): the hardest part to check, so we wrote out the routes the paper leaves to the reader.
- [11 Building the walls](#step-bl-build-walls): "since $q+1$ is odd" should be about $2q+1$.
- [12 Filling the strips](#step-bl-fill-strips): a typo, $q-k$ for $q-K$.
- [13 Untangling the linkers](#step-bl-crossbar): a typo, $10h-6$ for $10h-7$, and explicit paths for the two lemmas the paper proves only by figures.
- [14 Loops take up slack](#step-bl-linkers): the first linker starts at the spaced rod, which the paper does not mention.
- [15 Folding is NP-complete](#step-bl-conclusion): why bin packing must stay hard with small numbers.
