## The HP model

The HP model <Cite k="dill1985theory" /> reduces a protein to a string of H's (hydrophobic) and
P's (polar). It keeps one force of real folding: hydrophobic residues gather in a core.
[The front page](../) defines folds and contacts. <Cite k="istrail2009combinatorial" /> survey
the mathematics of the model up to 2009.

Before 1998, hardness was known only for models with more structure.
<Cite k="paterson1996complexity" /> proved it for an unbounded alphabet in which only identical
letters bond. Other proofs used energies that are part of the input <Cite k="unger1993finding" />,
angle rules on the diamond lattice <Cite k="ngo1992computational" />, or a graph of charges in
place of a chain <Cite k="fraenkel1993complexity" />. <Cite k="hart1997robust" /> extended such
results to every finitely representable lattice, still with an unbounded alphabet. In these models
the energy or the rules can pin down the shape of the fold. With two letters any two H's can form a
contact, so a reduction has to force the shape through the number of contacts alone. Unger and
Moult could not prove hardness for an HP-like model, and <Cite k="hart1996fast" /> listed the
complexity of HP folding as open and conjectured that it is NP-hard.

## Two proofs from 1998

In 1998 two independent papers proved HP folding NP-complete: <Cite k="berger1998protein" /> on
the cubic lattice and <Cite k="crescenzi1998complexity" /> on the square lattice. The second paper
credits Berger and Leighton with proving the cubic case a few months earlier.

Berger and Leighton reduce from a form of bin packing in which every bin must be exactly full. It is
strongly NP-complete, which follows from 3-PARTITION <Cite k="garey1979computers" />. Strong
hardness is needed because each item size becomes a length in the string, written in unary. The
string has $n^3$ H's, and $n^3$ H's have the most contacts exactly when they fill an
$n\times n\times n$ cube. Berger and Leighton cite this cube lemma as a classic result, without a
source. Its inequality is the discrete Loomis–Whitney inequality <Cite k="loomis1949inequality" />,
and <Cite k="atkins1999intractability" /> prove the lemma in full, with the case of equality.

In a perfect fold every P lies outside the cube, and short blocks such as <Seq s="PHP" /> are forced
onto its edges and faces. Some blocks build walls that cut one face into bins, and the blocks that
encode the items must fit into the bins. That a perfect fold gives a packing takes about a page and
a half of counting. The converse is longer. It needs explicit coordinates for the fold, and six
lemmas that route the **linkers**, the runs of P's that join the pieces of the string. The lemmas
are Berger and Leighton's own; they credit the general method to work on three-dimensional circuit
layout <Cite k="leighton1986three" />.

Crescenzi et al. reduce from Hamiltonian path. They measure a fold by its **losses**: lattice edges
that join an H to a node that is not an H and are not edges of the string. Fewer losses means more
contacts. The proof first folds a set of strings instead of one. Each vertex of the graph gets
strings that carry its codeword in a Hadamard-code embedding of the graph
<Cite k="trevisan1997when" />, where the Hamming distance between two codewords tells whether the
vertices are adjacent. Strings laid side by side in the order of a Hamiltonian path form a
near-square of H's, whose only losses come from mismatched codewords. The target is a positive
budget of $E = 7n(n-1)$ losses for a graph on $n$ vertices. The proof that a fold within this budget
gives a Hamiltonian path takes 11 pages of the journal version, plus 25 pages of appendices of local
case analysis. A second step joins the strings into one, starting from Hamiltonian cycle in a
special class of planar graphs; its converse is one sentence. A 3D version is sketched in one
paragraph.

| | Berger and Leighton | Crescenzi et al. |
|---|---|---|
| Lattice | cubic, $\mathbb Z^3$ | square, $\mathbb Z^2$ |
| Source problem | bin packing, strongly NP-complete | Hamiltonian path |
| Target | a perfect fold: the H's fill a cube | at most $E > 0$ losses |
| Main gadget | walls that cut a face of the cube into bins | Hadamard codewords for the vertices |
| Longest part | the fold and the routing of the linkers | fold ⇒ Hamiltonian path |
| Length of the journal version | 14 pages | 43 pages, 25 of them appendices |

## What came after

### Variants

Later hardness proofs change the problem. <Cite k="nayak1998spatial" /> use random codewords over
an alphabet of about $10^{300}$ letters, and also prove MAX SNP-hardness, both under randomized
reductions. They write that two letters would need a new idea.
<Cite k="atkins1999intractability" /> use 12 letters with a designed table of contact energies, and
give a deterministic proof on the cubic lattice. If the region to fill is part of the input, a
three-page reduction <Cite k="feng2022bicolored" /> from Hamiltonian path in grid graphs
<Cite k="itai1982hamilton" /> is enough. If the angle at every residue is fixed, hardness holds in
2D even with two H's <Cite k="demaine2022computational" />, and in 3D through filling a cube with
prescribed turns <Cite k="abel2013finding" />. In our reading, each change gives the reduction a
way to fix the shape other than the number of contacts.

### Approximation

<Cite k="hart1996fast" /> gave algorithms that reach, asymptotically, 1/4 of the optimal score in
2D and 3/8 in 3D. They rest on a parity bound: the lattices are bipartite, so an H can form
contacts only with H's whose positions in the string have the other parity.
<Cite k="istrail2009combinatorial" /> restate the 2D bound without its term for H's at the ends of
the string. That form fails for <Seq s="HPPHPHPH" />, which folds with 3 contacts against a bound
of 2 (our check).

<Cite k="berger1998protein" /> derive hardness within $9/8-\varepsilon$ for a related measure, the
number of missed H–H bonds: deciding whether a fold misses only the minimum of 8 is NP-hard, and
the count is an integer. <Cite k="crescenzi1998complexity" /> claim, without proof, that minimizing
losses in 3D is MAX SNP-complete. For the number of contacts itself, hardness of approximation is
open <Cite k="nayak1998spatial" /> <Cite k="demaine2012geometric" />.

### Inverse folding

The reverse problem asks for a string that folds well into a given shape.
<Cite k="hart1997computational" /> claimed that a canonical version is NP-complete on both
lattices. <Cite k="berman2004protein" /> showed that the 2D reduction is wrong, solved the 2D case
in polynomial time, and proved the 3D case NP-complete and gave a PTAS for it. It is the only
published hardness proof in the area that we know to be wrong. <Cite k="kleinberg1999efficient" />
solved another design model in polynomial time.

### Formalization

We found no machine-checked hardness proof for HP folding. The only formalization of the model we
found is in Lean: folds, the contact score, score bounds and a verified search for optimal folds
<Cite k="kjoshanssen2024ptyche" />. Mathlib, Lean's main library, has polynomial-time Turing
machines but no NP <Cite k="mathlib2026computability" />. Four newer Lean libraries add NP and
reductions <Cite k="cslibfrontier2026complexity" /> <Cite k="senellart2026descriptive" />
<Cite k="solresol2026nphardness" /> <Cite k="rbarish2026npcompleteness" />. They are large, three
of them were written with AI assistance, and we have not reviewed them. The Cook–Levin theorem is
formalized in Coq <Cite k="gaher2021mechanising" /> and in Isabelle <Cite k="balbach2023cook" />.
<Cite k="kreuzer2023verification" /> checked in Isabelle that NP-hardness reductions for the closest
and shortest vector problems are correct, and argued their running time on paper. Along the way
they found and fixed problems in the published proofs.

## The open problem

<Cite k="demaine2012geometric" /> skipped both proofs in his MIT lectures as complicated, and listed
finding "a simple or cleaner proof" as an open problem. The surveys restate the theorems:
<Cite k="istrail2009combinatorial" /> without proofs, and <Cite k="chandru2003algorithmics" /> with
a sketch of the 3D construction that leaves out the proof that a perfect fold gives a packing. We
searched the papers that cite either proof: 820 through Semantic Scholar and 653 through OpenAlex.
None gives a new or simpler proof for the two-letter model. We also found no erratum and no
independent check of either proof. The only published criticism, by <Cite k="bahi2013protein" />,
is about modelling: the 2D proof ranges over all self-avoiding walks, including folds that pivot
moves from the straight chain cannot reach. It does not dispute correctness.

Three related questions are also open in the literature:

- hardness of approximating the number of contacts <Cite k="nayak1998spatial" />
  <Cite k="demaine2012geometric" />;
- hardness on other lattices: we found no hardness proof for HP folding on the triangular,
  hexagonal or face-centred cubic lattice, where only approximation algorithms are known, and
  <Cite k="istrail2009combinatorial" /> list the hexagonal case as open;
- hardness for strings whose optimal fold is unique <Cite k="aichholzer2003long" />, which the known
  proofs do not cover.
