The short proof, *Protein folding in the HP model on the cubic lattice is NP-complete: a short proof*, keeps the idea of [the original proof](../original-proof/), a string whose H's can fill a cube exactly when the items can be packed, but encodes the instance on a single edge of the cube. Lone H's on that edge, the *separators*, cut it into bins; the items are runs of H's lying along it; and two runs of P's that are only just long enough to reach across a face pin the separators at the two ends, so that every bin is forced to be exactly full.

Here the construction runs on a computer, like [Berger and Leighton's](../folded/). Choose an instance and drag its items into the bins, or let the solver pack them. Then press **Fold the string**: $S$ appears as a straight chain, and every press of <kbd>→</kbd> folds its next piece as in Section 5 of the paper, until the H's fill an $n\times n\times n$ cube and the bottom front edge, the *shelf*, carries your packing.

## Reading the picture

The big red beads are the H's and the small grey ones the P's. The H's of the core are pale, those on the edges a stronger red, the separators dark, and every item has its own bright colour, the same as on the packing board. Brackets in front of the shelf mark the bins and name the items in them. The sun and moon button switches between day and night, and *View* offers one colour per piece of the string, hides the P's, or cuts a corner off the cube, which is the only way to watch the core fill the inside.

**Explain** opens the list of steps in the order of the string, what each step folds and why it is forced, and counters of the letters placed and the H–H pairs. The bar under the step's name is the whole string $S$; drag along it to fold or unfold to any letter, or step with <kbd>→</kbd> and <kbd>←</kbd>. Drag the cube to turn it and zoom with <kbd>+</kbd> and <kbd>−</kbd>; scrolling scrolls the page.

## The string

Before anything else the instance is normalised: $B$ and every size are multiplied by 6, and if the number of bins $m$ is even, one item of size $B$ is added with a bin of its own, so that $m$ becomes odd. Then $n = m(B+1)+3$ and, with $k$ items of sizes $a_1,\dots,a_k$,

$$
S = H^{N_0}\,PP\,D_{9n-10}\,P^{\lambda}\,D_{n-2}\,P^{n}\,H(P^{B+6}H)^{m}\,P^{n}\,D_{n-2}\,P^{\lambda}D_{a_1}\cdots P^{\lambda}D_{a_k}\,P,
$$

where $D_r = H(PPH)^{r-1}$ is a *dense run* of $r$ H's, each between two P's, $N_0 = n^3-12n+16$ and $\lambda = 4n+2k$. Every H of a dense run, and every separator, has P's on both sides, so in a perfect fold it lies on an edge of the cube. There are exactly $12n-16$ of them, as many as the edges have nodes, and the core $H^{N_0}$ fills the rest.

Even the smallest instances give a sizeable cube: two bins of size 2 become three bins of size 12, so $n = 42$, with 74,088 H's and about 76,000 letters. One bin of size 2 already gives $n = 16$, which is among the presets.

## How close this is to the paper

The string is exactly that of Section 3, checked letter by letter, and the core, the runs, the tethers and the items sit where Table 1 of the paper puts them: the core layer by layer as in Lemma 14, the dense runs with their staples as in Lemma 13. One kind of route we had to choose ourselves. Each linker $P^{\lambda}$ goes straight down to a level of its own below the cube and comes back up, as in the Routing Lemma, but the paper uses up its surplus length with a rectangle in front of the cube. Here the surplus is folded into a flat meander under the cube, on the same level, so that nothing hangs in front of the shelf.

Every fold is checked as it is built: its letters are those of $S$, no site is used twice, the H's fill the cube, and the H–H pairs reach $3n^2(n-1)$. The panel on the left lists these checks.
