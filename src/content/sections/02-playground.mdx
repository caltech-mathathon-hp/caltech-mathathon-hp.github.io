Place the letters of the string one at a time, each on a free node next to the one before. Two H's
that end up side by side without being neighbours in the string make a **contact**, drawn as a
dashed line. Each level says how many contacts its best fold has; try to reach that number. The
[front page](../) explains the model behind the game.

## Levels and lattices

We start on the cubic lattice, where Berger and Leighton proved their theorem. Its five levels lead
from a first contact to a cube of 27 H's, and the sections below say what they show. Show solution
plays a best fold, which you can then change like your own. The switch at the top of the game
changes between 3D and 2D, and the tabs beside it change the lattice. The numbered chips under them
pick a level, and under Own string you can fold any string you type.

## What the cubic levels teach

Three ideas of Berger and Leighton's proof show up in the levels on the cubic lattice.

### H's gather into a cube

Every side of an H that does not touch another H is a lost contact, so the score grows as the H's
gather and the P's move outside. On the cubic lattice they gather best into a cube, because a set
of $n^3$ nodes has the most neighbouring pairs only when it is an $n \times n \times n$ cube.
*Eight H's* shows this for $n = 2$: its best fold has 5 contacts, and only a 2×2×2 cube of H's
reaches 5. *The 3×3×3 cube* does the same for $n = 3$, with 28 contacts. Berger and Leighton's
strings have $n^3$ H's, so for them too no other shape of the H's has as many contacts as the cube,
and a fold with that many fills the cube with H's. The [front page](../) calls such a fold perfect.

### P's push H's to the surface

A perfect fold leaves no room in the cube for P's, so every P sits outside it. In *A core with
loops* each PP loop therefore leaves the cube and comes back next to where it left. In a larger
cube this decides where each H can go, because the letters beside an H in the string sit on
neighbouring nodes. An H between two P's, as in <Seq s="PHP" />, has two sides facing out of the
cube, so it sits on an edge or at a corner. The H's of <Seq s="PHHP" /> each have one side facing
out, and the counting in the proof puts them on a face. Berger and Leighton spell their string with
such pieces: rods such as <Seq s="(PHP)^3" /> go along the edges, and ribbons such as
<Seq s="(PHHP)^3" /> go on the faces. [The original proof](../original-proof/) builds its bins on
this frame of edges and faces.

### Early choices decide late ones

In *The 3×3×3 cube* the fold must pass through every node of the cube, so a choice made early can
make the cube impossible to close much later. Even the first letter matters. Colour the nodes of
the cube with two colours so that neighbours differ: the 8 corners and the 6 face centres get one
colour, the 12 edge midpoints and the centre the other. Consecutive letters alternate colours, so
the 14 letters at odd positions all share a colour, and only the first colour has 14 nodes. A fold
that starts in the middle of an edge, or at the centre, can therefore never fill the cube.

On the cubic lattice each new letter has up to five free directions, so the folds multiply: their
number grows like $4.68^n$ for a string of
$n+1$ letters, and folds with many contacts are rare among them. The [front page](../) explains why
many folds alone do not make a problem hard.

## NP-complete on two lattices, open on four

The switch and the tabs at the top of the game move it to five other lattices, and the same string
can score differently on each. *The 20-letter test string* is a level on every lattice but the
face-centred cubic one: its best fold has 11 contacts on the cubic lattice, 9 on the square, 15 on
the triangular, 6 on the diamond and 4 on the hexagonal. On each lattice we can ask the
[question on the front page](../): does a string have a fold with at least a given number of
contacts? The table says what is known. "Open" means that we found no proof of hardness
([Context](../context/)).

| Lattice | Neighbours of a node | What is known |
|---|---|---|
| Cubic (3D) | 6 | NP-complete <Cite k="berger1998protein" /> |
| Face-centred cubic (3D) | 12 | open |
| Diamond (3D) | 4 | open; a path problem with rules on dihedral angles is NP-complete <Cite k="ngo1992computational" /> |
| Square (2D) | 4 | NP-complete <Cite k="crescenzi1998complexity" /> |
| Triangular (2D) | 6 | open; a 6/11-approximation is known <Cite k="istrail2009combinatorial" /> |
| Hexagonal (2D) | 3 | open, as listed by <Cite k="istrail2009combinatorial" /> |

The cubic, diamond, square and hexagonal lattices are bipartite: colour the nodes with two colours
so that neighbours differ, and every step of a fold changes colour. So two H's can touch only if
they are an odd number of steps apart along the string. The game's upper bound uses this. The
levels called *Odd and even* show the difference: <Seq s="HPHPHPHPH" /> has no contact on the square
lattice, but 7 on the triangular lattice and 8 on the face-centred cubic one, which have triangles.

## Bin packing, the puzzle behind the proof

**Bin packing** gives us some items, each with a size, and some bins of equal size, and asks
whether the items fill the bins exactly. It is the puzzle that Berger and Leighton translate into
folding. For each bin packing puzzle they write down one HP string, and a perfect fold of that
string exists exactly when the items fit.

We keep every size even, as Berger and Leighton do, because their string writes an item of size $s$
as $s/2$ copies of <Seq s="PHHP" />, a ribbon with as many H's as the item's size. On the front face
of the cube, walls cut out strips that act as the bins, and the only freedom a perfect fold has
left is which strip each item's ribbon goes into. For the last level, *The running example*, the
items fit, so the string has a perfect fold, and its H's fill a cube of 80×80×80.

Early choices decide late ones here too, as in *The 3×3×3 cube*: in *A dead end*, some first moves
leave no way to finish.
